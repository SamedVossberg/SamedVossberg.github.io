Neural Decoding: From Single Cells to Brain-Computer Interfaces

Published: June 2025 | Tags: Neuroscience, Neural Decoding, BCI, Machine Learning

Neural decoding is all about reading signals from the brain and translating them into something useful. What started with simple recordings from single neurons has evolved into sophisticated systems that can control robotic arms just by thinking about movements. In this post I will walk you through the major milestones and papers that shaped this field.

Single neuron recordings

In the late 1950s and early 1960s, researchers made the first systematic recordings from single neurons in behaving animals. These early studies showed that individual neurons could encode specific information about movements and sensory events. The idea was straightforward: if you record from enough neurons simultaneously, you can get a fairly good picture of what the brain is trying to do.

Linear decoding and the population vector

The groundbreaking work by Georgopoulos and colleagues in the 1980s showed that you can use simple linear methods to decode movement direction from the motor cortex. They introduced the population vector algorithm, which essentially takes the preferred direction of each neuron and weights it by the neuron's firing rate.

This was a big deal because it showed that you do not need complex nonlinear models to extract meaningful information from neural populations. You can find their seminal 1986 paper in Science: Georgopoulos et al., Science 83: 1416-1419 (1986). Their follow up work in the late 1980s established the population coding framework: Georgopoulos et al., Journal of Neuroscience 8: 2928-2937 (1988).

Brain computer interfaces take off

By the late 1990s and early 2000s, the field was moving fast. Several labs showed that you could use neural signals to control external devices in real time.

A major milestone came from the Donoghue lab at Brown University. They showed that paralyzed patients could control a computer cursor just by thinking about movement. Their seminal 2006 Nature paper with Leigh Hochberg remains one of the most cited works in the clinical BCI field. You can read about their BrainGate system here: Hochberg et al., Nature 442: 164-171 (2006).

Deep learning and neural decoding

About ten years ago, machine learning started changing how we decode neural activity. Traditional methods based on linear models and simple Kalman filters were good but limited. Deep neural networks opened up new possibilities for handling the complexity and noise in neural data.

The DeepLabCut paper by Mathis and colleagues in 2018 showed that deep learning could be used for markerless pose estimation from video. While not strictly neural decoding, this work demonstrated that CNNs could extract precise behavioral information from messy data. Check out their paper: Mathis et al., Nature Neuroscience 21: 1281-1289 (2018). Their 2020 Nature Methods paper extended this to 3D tracking across species: Mathis et al., Nature Methods 17: 944-946 (2020).

Where we are now

Today neural decoding is used in clinical trials for paralyzed patients and has helped restore movement to people with spinal cord injuries. Recent work using recurrent neural networks and transformers is pushing decoding accuracy even higher. We are now at a point where artificial limbs controlled by thought alone are becoming a reality for patients who have lost limbs.

The field has come a long way from single neuron recordings to real time brain computer interfaces. What was once science fiction is now entering clinical practice.
